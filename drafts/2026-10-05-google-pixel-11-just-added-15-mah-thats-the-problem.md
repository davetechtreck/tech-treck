The [Google Pixel 11](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Google+Pixel+11&tag=techtreck02-20)'s battery is 4,985 mAh. The Pixel 10's battery was 4,970 mAh. That's a 15 mAh difference, or about 0.3 percent. It's the only real upgrade, and it isn't one.

Google announced the Pixel 11 in August and positioned it as the next step forward for its flagship line. But when you compare spec sheets, the phone is virtually identical to what shipped a year ago. Same 6.3-inch display. Same 30W wired charging. Same 12 GB of RAM. The processor bumped from Tensor G5 to G6, but that's a chip generation update, not a fundamental rethink. The battery got that tiny bump. That's it.

The test results don't tell a different story. GSMArena's active use score improved from 12 hours and 8 minutes on the Pixel 10 to 15 hours and 40 minutes on the Pixel 11—better, sure, but that's software optimization and a slightly more efficient chip doing the work, not a meaningfully larger power reserve. The EU endurance rating actually dropped slightly, from 49 hours and 23 minutes to 48 hours and 50 minutes. Real-world battery life is a wash.

This is a phone you should skip unless your current device is actively dying. If your Pixel 9 still works, there's nothing here. If you're on a Pixel 10, you're looking at a lateral move with a new model number. Even if you're coming from something older, the Pixel 10 is now cheaper and functionally the same phone.

The problem isn't that the Pixel 11 is bad. It's that it's boring. Google's annual release cycle used to mean something—new camera tricks, better displays, battery jumps that mattered. This year, the company shipped a spec-bump refresh and called it a flagship. The 15 mAh battery increase is a perfect metaphor: technically an upgrade, but so small it's almost insulting to call it one.

Wait for the Pixel 12, or buy last year's model at a discount. Either way, the Pixel 11 isn't worth your money right now.
