---
title: The Pentagon Is About to Become a VC for AI Data Centers
date: 2026-09-12
tags: ai infrastructure, defense, data centers
snippet: A $5B loan to Fluidstack marks DoD's most direct play yet in private AI infrastructure—advised by Palmer Luckey's new bank.
---

The Pentagon is in talks to lend roughly $5 billion to Fluidstack, an AI cloud-computing startup you've probably never heard of, to shore up the U.S. data-center supply chain. If the deal goes through, it will mark the Defense Department's most direct move yet into financing private AI infrastructure buildout.

Let that sink in for a moment. The United States military is about to become a creditor to a startup in the business of renting GPUs. Not through DARPA grants or R&D contracts or the usual procurement theater, but through a direct loan that looks a lot like venture debt with a national-security wrapper.

The advisory work is being handled by Erebor Bank, the hard-tech lender Palmer Luckey opened in February 2026 that has already pulled in more than $4.6 billion in deposits. That's not a typo: a seven-month-old bank founded by the guy who sold Oculus to Facebook and then started a defense contractor is now advising the Pentagon on AI infrastructure finance.

This is what happens when the AI boom collides with supply-chain anxiety. Microsoft just mapped out 38 gigawatts of future data-center capacity. Oracle is sitting on a $664 billion cloud backlog. And the U.S. government has apparently decided it can't afford to let domestic data-center buildout fall behind because every AI lab decided to light billions on fire training models at the same time.

Fluidstack itself is a relatively small player in a market dominated by hyperscalers. But that's exactly the point. The Pentagon isn't trying to pick a winner in the cloud wars. It's trying to make sure there are enough domestic options that the defense industrial base doesn't end up bottlenecked by whoever controls GPU allocation at AWS or Azure.

The real question is what happens when this loan becomes a template. If DoD can lend $5 billion to shore up data-center capacity, what stops Treasury or Energy from doing the same? We've already seen governments subsidize chip fabs. Now we're subsidizing the buildings that run the chips. At some point we should probably admit we're running industrial policy and stop pretending it's just national security.
