Nvidia's [GeForce RTX 5090](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=GeForce+RTX+5090&tag=techtreck02-20) has officially entered the realm of the absurd. The flagship gaming graphics card, which launched at $1,999, is now showing up on third-party marketplaces at prices approaching $10,000. Major retailers like Amazon, Newegg, and Best Buy are either completely out of stock or listing cards through marketplace sellers at $6,000 to $9,500, depending on the model.

This isn't a short-term scalping spike. It's a full-blown supply crisis driven by AI demand colliding with a GDDR7 memory shortage. When cards do appear at official retailers, they're gone in minutes, typically priced between $4,400 and $5,000. That's already more than double the launch MSRP, and those are the *good* deals.

The progression has been brutal. In June, median prices hovered around $4,299. By early September, the floor had climbed to roughly $5,199. Now, just two weeks later, standard retail inventory has essentially disappeared. One recent Newegg listing for an [ASUS TUF Gaming RTX 5090 OC](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=ASUS+TUF+Gaming+RTX+5090+OC&tag=techtreck02-20) hit $9,299. Some sellers are pushing past the $10,000 mark.

AI buyers are the obvious culprit. The RTX 5090's 32GB of VRAM and raw compute power make it attractive for commercial AI work, where the card can theoretically generate revenue. Gamers paying these prices, meanwhile, are simply spending eye-watering amounts for higher frame rates. Reports suggest AI companies have been buying RTX 5090s by the pallet, absorbing retail inventory almost immediately after restocks.

Physical retail remains one of the few realistic options. Micro Center locations reportedly still have stock, with the cheapest models around $4,299. That's still more than double MSRP, but it beats the online chaos. The retailer limits high-demand GPU purchases to in-store transactions, making large-scale bulk buying harder.

For context, this makes the 2021 crypto mining boom look almost reasonable. Back then, RTX 3090s topped out around $3,000 to $4,000 on the secondary market. The RTX 5090 is seeing a 190 percent markup from launch price in some cases, with no clear end in sight.

The [RTX 5080](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=RTX+5080&tag=techtreck02-20) remains available at its $999 MSRP and delivers roughly 75 to 80 percent of the 5090's gaming performance at 4K. For anyone building a gaming PC right now, that's the default answer. The alternative is paying prebuilt pricing, where a complete system with an RTX 5090 might cost $7,500 but at least includes everything else you need. It's backwards economics, but when the GPU alone costs $9,000 on eBay, a $7,500 prebuilt starts looking rational.

Nvidia hasn't stopped manufacturing the card. The problem is structural: constrained GB202 die supply, memory shortages, and datacenter demand competing for the same production lines. With the next-generation RTX 60 series reportedly delayed until 2028, the RTX 5090 shortage could persist for years, not months. If you want one for gaming, you're either camping Micro Center or paying the scalper tax. There's no good third option right now.
