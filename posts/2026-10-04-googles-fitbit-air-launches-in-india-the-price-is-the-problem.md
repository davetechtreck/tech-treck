Google just launched the [Fitbit Air](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Fitbit+Air&tag=techtreck02-20) in India, and the whole point was supposed to be accessibility. A stripped-down fitness tracker for people who don't need GPS, don't want to pay for premium features, and just want step counts and sleep data that sync to their phone. Clean concept. But Google priced it in a way that might keep it from finding the audience it was designed for.

The Fitbit Air is Google's attempt to go truly budget in a market where Xiaomi and Realme own the entry-level wearables category. No always-on display, no onboard GPS, no altimeter. What you get is continuous heart rate monitoring, basic sleep tracking, smartphone notifications, and roughly seven days of battery life. It's lighter than the Charge series, uses a simpler band mechanism, and skips the color screen in favor of a monochrome OLED that's easier to read in direct sunlight but feels like a step back if you've used anything recent.

The pitch makes sense. India is one of the biggest wearables markets in the world, and the sub-$50 segment is where the volume lives. Brands like Noise, boAt, and Fire-Boltt have flooded that space with trackers that cost less than dinner and do just enough to justify wearing them. Google needed something that could compete on price while still carrying the Fitbit name and the Google Health integration that actually works.

But here's where it falls apart: Google is asking too much. Early reports suggest the Fitbit Air is priced higher than comparable local options that offer similar specs and, in some cases, better displays. The Fitbit app is more polished, the data accuracy is likely better, and you're buying into an ecosystem that connects to Google Fit and works across Android and iOS. But in a market this price-sensitive, "better software" doesn't move units the way a lower sticker price does.

Google has a history of misjudging what budget actually means. The Pixel A-series keeps inching up in price. The Nest Mini launched higher than the Echo Dot. And now the Fitbit Air arrives in India with a premium that doesn't match the product's intentionally limited feature set. If the goal was market share, the price needed to be aggressive. If the goal was margin, they should have added the features to justify it.

The Fitbit Air will find buyers—people who want the Google Health connection, existing Fitbit users stepping down from a Charge or Versa, and anyone who values data accuracy over flashy specs. But in a country where wearables are impulse buys and brand loyalty is thin, Google just made it harder for itself. Again.
