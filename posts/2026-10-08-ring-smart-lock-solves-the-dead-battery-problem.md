For anyone who's owned a smart lock, the dread is real: the battery dies while you're out, and you're locked out of your own house. Most locks try to prevent this with a physical key backup or external battery port. Ring just said no to both.

The [Ring Smart Lock](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Ring+Smart+Lock&tag=techtreck02-20) replaces the keyhole with a dial on the front that doubles as a hand-crank generator. Turn it five to ten times, and you generate enough power for about 30 seconds—enough to unlock the door. The supercapacitor inside doesn't store charge for long, which means this is genuinely an emergency feature, not a second unlock method. That's the point: you only reach for it when the main battery is actually dead.

The day-to-day experience is conventional. The lock supports fingerprint entry, a backlit touchscreen keypad, the Ring app, and Alexa commands. The removable Quick Release Ultra Battery Pack lasts about a year between swaps. It fits any standard single-cylinder deadbolt, needs no extra wiring, and comes in black, silver, and brass.

Ring built most of this from off-the-shelf parts—the hand-crank motor, the supercapacitor, the fingerprint reader. What's new is the integration: squeezing emergency mechanical power into a compact package and deciding that this, not a backup key, is what differentiates you in a crowded category. The company positioned it as a feature directly answering one of smart-lock anxiety's most persistent questions.

The lock relies on Amazon Sidewalk, the mesh network Ring builds through nearby Amazon devices, so remote unlocking doesn't depend on your home Wi-Fi. Nothing requires a Ring subscription.

Ring Smart Lock costs $249 and launches in the U.S. in Q1 2027, pending FCC approval. It's not here yet, but it's worth watching—both for people actually shopping smart locks and for how other manufacturers respond to the hand-crank idea. If the dial works as advertised, it removes one legitimate reason to avoid going keyless.
