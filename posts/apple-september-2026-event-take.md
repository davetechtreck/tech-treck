# Apple's September Event Is Betting Big — And I'm Not Sure the Bet Pays Off

Apple holds its iPhone event tomorrow, September 9, and for once the leaks haven't just hinted at "faster chip, better camera." This year Apple is reportedly skipping the standard iPhone 18 entirely and going all-in on three premium devices: the iPhone 18 Pro, the iPhone 18 Pro Max, and a foldable that could be called the iPhone Ultra. Having sat with the rumor mill for a few weeks now, I've got some opinions — and not all of them are charitable.

## The foldable is the real headline, and it's overdue

Let's start with the obvious: Apple is finally making a foldable phone. Every other major manufacturer has spent the better part of five years iterating on fold mechanisms, hinge durability, and crease-reduction, and Apple has just... watched. The rumored design — a book-style fold with a roughly 5.5-inch outer display opening into a ~7.8-inch inner OLED — sounds like Apple did its homework rather than rushing a first-gen product out the door.

That's the optimistic read. The skeptical read is that Apple traditionally doesn't ship a category until it's confident it can define it, and "confident" for Apple has historically meant "expensive." Reports point to a starting price in the $2,000–$2,300 range for the base model. If that holds, this isn't a phone for early adopters who want to try folding screens — it's a phone for people who've already decided price is irrelevant. I don't think that's a knock on Apple so much as an acknowledgment of what "Apple does foldables" was always going to mean.

## Killing the standard iPhone 18 is the more interesting decision

The quieter story here — no standard iPhone 18 this cycle, with the affordable lineup (iPhone 18 and 18e) pushed to spring 2027 — is arguably a bigger strategic swing than the foldable itself. Apple is effectively splitting its iPhone calendar into two events a year instead of one, which is either a smart way to spread manufacturing and marketing load across the calendar, or a sign that supply constraints (memory shortages keep getting cited) are forcing Apple's hand rather than this being some grand design decision.

I lean toward the latter. When a company known for ruthless roadmap discipline suddenly reshuffles a decade-old release cadence, "we planned this" and "we had no choice" tend to produce identical announcements. The memory shortage angle floating around from analysts feels like the more honest explanation, even if it never gets said from the stage.

## Price increases are coming, and this is the one prediction I'd bet on

KeyBanc's research note flagging this event as a potential negative catalyst for the stock is worth paying attention to, and not because Wall Street is always right about Apple hardware (it usually isn't). It's worth paying attention to because the logic is simple: component costs are up, especially memory, and Apple has two levers — margin or price. Apple almost never pulls the margin lever.

If the iPhone 18 Pro line comes in $200–$300 above the outgoing iPhone 17 Pro pricing, that won't be a surprise to anyone who's watched Apple's pricing behavior over the last few cycles. What will be worth watching is whether Apple tries to frame that increase as being "for" the foldable — bundling the price story of the flagship Pro line together with the halo device, so the premium feels justified by association rather than by what the Pro phones themselves actually gained.

## The A20 Pro and the parts nobody will talk about on stage

Buried under the foldable hype is the more consequential engineering story: all three new iPhones reportedly move to the A20 Pro on a 2nm process. That's the kind of upgrade that doesn't sell keynote seats but actually matters for battery life, sustained performance, and how these phones age over three or four years of ownership. If I were giving advice rather than opinions, I'd say the process node shrink is the thing worth caring about — the fold is the thing worth talking about at dinner.

## My actual take

Apple doesn't need this event to be a home run. The base is going to upgrade regardless of what ships, and the foldable doesn't need to sell millions of units in year one — it needs to exist, get iterated on, and let Apple say "we were deliberate" the way it always does with new categories. What I'll be watching for tomorrow isn't whether the foldable is good. It's whether Apple can justify the pricing story with anything more substantive than "it's Apple, and it folds."

If John Ternus's "huge launch" teaser is honest marketing rather than standard hype-cycle language, we'll find out at 10 a.m. Pacific tomorrow.
